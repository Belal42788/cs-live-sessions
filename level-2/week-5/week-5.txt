Topic 5.1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence
Understanding how computers learn from experience instead of following fixed rules.

Think about how you learned to ride a bicycle. Nobody gave you a step-by-step instruction manual. You tried, you wobbled, you fell a few times, and eventually your brain figured out the pattern of balance. Artificial Intelligence works in a similar way.

Traditional computer programs are like recipes. They follow exact steps in a specific order. If you skip step three, the whole thing breaks. But AI programs are different. Most of them learn from examples, just like you learned to ride that bike.

ℹ️
The Big Idea
AI refers to systems that can perform tasks requiring human-like intelligence. Many modern AI systems learn patterns from data, allowing them to improve their performance without being explicitly programmed for every situation.

Traditional programs follow fixed rules, so they produce predictable outputs for a given input. For example, 2 + 2 will always equal 4.

Many AI systems, on the other hand, use probabilities to make decisions. When an AI analyzes a photo and says “this is probably a cat,” it is estimating how likely that prediction is based on patterns it learned from data.

This ability to handle uncertainty helps AI work with complex, real-world situations where answers are not always clear, although it can also lead to occasional mistakes.

Deterministic logic always produces the same answer for the same input. Probabilistic reasoning makes its best educated guess and quantifies its confidence.

Classical AI — also called Rule-Based Systems or Symbolic AI — is the older, original approach to building intelligent machines. The idea is simple: a human expert sits down and writes down every rule the computer should follow, usually in the form of IF–THEN statements. The computer doesn't learn anything on its own; it just follows the rules its programmer gave it.

For example, an early medical diagnosis program might contain rules like: IF the patient has a fever AND a cough AND a sore throat, THEN suggest the flu. If the doctor who wrote the rules forgot a symptom, the program would simply miss that case.

Advantages of Classical AI
Transparent and explainable: You can read the rules and understand exactly why the system made a decision.
Predictable: The same input always produces the same output.
No training data required: The system works once the rules are defined, without needing large datasets.
Effective in well-defined domains: Works best in areas with clear rules, such as tax systems, configuration systems, or simple decision-making tasks.
Disadvantages of Classical AI
Rigid: Struggles with situations not explicitly covered by the rules.
Hard to scale: Complex real-world problems may require a very large number of rules.
Expensive to maintain: Adding new cases or updating knowledge requires manual effort from experts.
Limited handling of uncertainty: Basic rule-based systems do not naturally handle noisy or ambiguous data without additional techniques.
Data-Driven AI — also called Learning-Based Systems or Machine Learning — is the modern approach. Instead of asking a human to write every rule, we give the computer thousands of examples and let it discover the rules on its own. The computer looks at the data, finds the patterns, and builds its own internal model of how the world works.

For example, instead of writing rules to identify the flu, we show the computer ten thousand patient records — some with the flu, some without — and let it figure out which combinations of symptoms are most predictive. The computer learns the rules; we never write them.

Advantages of Data-Driven AI
Handles complexity: Can learn patterns no human could ever write down by hand.
Improves with more data: The more examples it sees, the better it gets.
Generalizes: Can make sensible decisions about new situations it has never seen before.
Flexible: The same approach works for images, text, audio, sensor data — almost anything.
Disadvantages of Data-Driven AI
Requires lots of data: Needs many high-quality examples to learn well.
Black box: It is often hard to explain exactly why the system made a particular decision.
Computationally expensive: Training large models takes significant time and hardware.
Inherits biases from data: If the training data is biased, the model will be biased too.
Quick Comparison

How does it improve?	A human edits the rules	It sees more data

Early AI researchers tried to teach computers by writing down every single rule. They thought that if they could just write enough rules, the computer would be smart. This worked okay for things like chess, where all the rules are clear and fixed.

But the real world doesn't work that way. How would you write rules for recognizing your friend's face? Would you measure the distance between their eyes? The shape of their nose? What about when they're wearing sunglasses or standing in shadow? You'd need millions of rules, and you'd still miss something.

Modern AI takes a different approach. Instead of writing the rules ourselves, we show the computer thousands of examples and let it discover the patterns on its own. This is called the data-driven approach, and it's what makes today's AI so powerful.

Worked Example: Filtering Spam Email
Imagine we need to build a system that decides whether an incoming email is spam or not. Let's see how the same problem is solved with each of the two approaches.

Solution 1 — Classical AI (Rule-Based)
A human engineer sits down and writes a list of explicit rules:

IF the email contains the word "lottery" OR "winner" OR "free money" → mark as spam.
IF the sender's address is not in the user's contact list AND the email has more than three exclamation marks → mark as spam.
IF the email contains the phrase "click here urgently" → mark as spam.
This works for the cases the engineer thought of. But the moment a spammer writes "l0ttery" with a zero, or uses a brand-new phrase, the rules fail. The engineer has to keep adding new rules forever, and the list grows uncontrollably.

Solution 2 — Data-Driven AI (Learning-Based)
Instead of writing rules, we collect a large dataset: say, fifty thousand emails that have already been labeled as either "spam" or "not spam" by human users. We feed all of them to a Machine Learning algorithm and let it figure out the patterns on its own.

The model learns automatically that certain words, phrasing styles, sender behaviours, and even sentence-length patterns are statistically associated with spam. When a brand-new email arrives — even one with words the engineer never anticipated — the model can still make a sensible probability estimate of whether it's spam, because it learned the underlying patterns rather than memorizing a fixed list.

✅
Same problem, two very different approaches
The Classical AI solution depends entirely on the human writing good rules. The Data-Driven AI solution depends entirely on the quality and quantity of the data. Modern email providers use the data-driven approach because spam evolves too quickly for any rule list to keep up.

Every AI system does two important things. First, it finds patterns in data. Second, it applies those patterns to new situations it hasn't seen before.

Imagine showing an AI a thousand pictures of cats. It starts noticing things like pointy ears, whiskers, and certain eye shapes. Now when you show it a new cat picture, it recognizes these familiar patterns and says 'that's a cat!' This ability to recognize patterns in new situations is called generalization.

ℹ️
The Memorization Trap
Sometimes AI systems cheat by memorizing specific examples instead of learning general patterns. When this happens, they fail on anything new. This problem is called overfitting.

Topic 5.2: Different Fields of AI and Their Real-Life Applications
Exploring the different ways AI understands the world around it.

Imagine an ordinary morning. Your phone's alarm wakes you up. You unlock the screen with your face — that's Computer Vision. You ask your voice assistant about the weather; it understands you and answers — that's Natural Language Processing. On the way out, your robot vacuum is already cleaning the living room, navigating around the chairs by itself — that's Robotics. And as you cross the street, a self-driving car pauses for you because its cameras saw you, its microphones heard the traffic, and its motors decided to brake — that's Multimodal AI, combining several abilities at once.

In just a few minutes you've already touched all four major flavours of modern AI without even thinking about it. They show up in the apps you use, the cars on the road, the hospitals that diagnose patients, the factories that build products, and the games you play.

Each area focuses on a different type of task, although they often overlap and work together.

Computer Vision deals with visual data such as images and videos.
Natural Language Processing focuses on understanding and generating human language.
Robotics involves machines that interact with the physical world, often using AI to perceive and act.
Multimodal AI combines multiple types of data—such as vision, language, and audio—so a system can process information in a more integrated way.
In the rest of this topic, we’ll explore each of these in turn—what they do, where you encounter them in daily life, and how they fit into the broader landscape of modern AI.

Each AI modality specialises in a different kind of perception or action. Multimodal AI brings them together into a single system that can see, hear, and act at the same time.

When you look at a photo of your dog, you instantly see your dog. You recognize the wagging tail, the floppy ears, the happy expression. Your brain does this automatically without any effort.

Computers see the same photo very differently. To a computer, an image is just a giant grid of numbers. Each tiny square in the grid (called a pixel) has a number that represents its color. A photo of your dog might be made of millions of these numbers arranged in rows and columns.

Computer Vision is the field that teaches computers to make sense of these numbers. Computer Vision algorithms can look at those millions of numbers and figure out that they form the pattern of a dog. They can even tell you where in the photo the dog is located and what the dog is doing.

Computer Vision in Daily Life
Computer Vision is already running quietly in the background of countless tools you use every day:

Face Recognition — When your phone unlocks the moment you look at it, Computer Vision is comparing the live image of your face to the one stored at setup. The same technology powers automatic photo-tagging in social media and lets airports speed up boarding by matching your face to your passport photo.
Self-Driving Cars — Cameras on the car capture the road, other vehicles, traffic signs, lane lines, and pedestrians. Computer Vision interprets all of this in real time so the car can decide whether to accelerate, brake, or turn safely.
Medical Image Analysis — Doctors use AI tools that scan X-rays, MRIs, and CT scans to spot tumours, fractures, or other early signs of disease. The system flags suspicious areas, helping radiologists focus on the cases most likely to need attention.
You're reading these words right now and understanding them automatically. But computers don't naturally understand language. They need help turning words into something they can work with, which is mathematics.

Natural Language Processing, or NLP for short, is how computers handle human language. The first step is converting words into numbers that represent their meaning. This process is called text vectorization. Once words become numbers, AI can start to understand relationships between them.

NLP powers many things you use every day. When you ask your phone a question and it understands you, that's NLP. When a website translates text from English to Arabic, that's NLP. When an app can tell if a review is positive or negative, that's also NLP.

Computer Vision and NLP help computers understand the world. Robotics is about taking action in the world. This is where AI moves from thinking to doing.

A robot arm in a factory uses AI to decide how to move. A self-driving car uses AI to control the steering wheel and brakes. A drone uses AI to stay balanced in the air. All of these are examples of AI systems that interact physically with their environment.

The most advanced robots combine multiple abilities. They use cameras to see (Computer Vision), microphones to hear (Speech Recognition), and motors to move (Robotics). When all these abilities work together, robots can navigate complex environments and respond to what's happening around them.

You use multiple senses at once all the time. When you're having a conversation with a friend, you see their facial expressions, hear their words, and notice their body language. Your brain combines all of this information to fully understand what they mean.

The most advanced AI systems work the same way. They're called multimodal systems because they use multiple modes of understanding. A self-driving car, for example, uses cameras to see the road, sensors to detect distances, and maps to understand where it's going. By combining all this information, it can make better decisions than if it only used one type of sensor.

Topic 5.3: Learning Paradigms: From Rules to Data
From writing every rule by hand to letting computers discover patterns on their own.

Picture two friends, Layla and Omar, who both want to teach a young child to recognize cats and dogs at the park.

Layla writes a careful list. "A dog has a long snout. A cat has pointy ears. Dogs are usually bigger. Cats meow." She reads the list to the child every day. The child memorises the rules and tries to apply them. When the dog at the park is small and fluffy and the cat is large and grey, the child gets confused — that case wasn't in the list.

Omar tries something different. He just walks the child to the park and points: "That's a dog. That's a dog. That's a cat. That's a dog." Day after day, the child sees hundreds of examples. Slowly, without ever being told a single rule, the child starts to know which is which. Even brand-new breeds the child has never seen before are easy to classify, because the child has built up an intuition from many examples.

This little story captures the entire shift this topic is about. Layla's approach is the old way of building AI: a human expert sits down and writes every rule. Omar's approach is the new way: we show the system many examples and let it learn the rules on its own.

In the rest of this topic we'll explore four dimensions of this shift:

Where do the rules come from? — handwritten by experts, or learned from data?
How does the system handle situations it has never seen before?
What happens when the world changes? — does the system need a human to update it, or does it adapt by itself when shown new examples?
Why did this shift change everything about the kinds of AI we can build today?
Keep Layla and Omar in mind as we go. The whole topic is just the deeper story behind the choice they made.

In the early days of AI, researchers had a simple idea. They thought intelligence was just a matter of knowing the right rules. So they tried to write down every single rule a smart system would need.

These systems were called expert systems, and they worked like giant decision trees. For example, a medical expert system might have hundreds of 'if-then' rules: 'If the patient has a fever AND a cough AND difficulty breathing, THEN consider pneumonia.' Doctors would work with programmers to write down every bit of medical knowledge they could think of.

This approach is called Symbolic AI, and it worked well in some situations. Chess-playing computers were incredibly good because chess has clear, fixed rules. The computer didn't need to learn anything. It just followed the rules people programmed into it.

The rule-based approach had a serious problem. It worked fine in neat, organized situations like board games or math problems. But the real world is messy and unpredictable.

Imagine trying to write rules for something as simple as recognizing a cat in a photo. You might start with 'if it has pointy ears and whiskers, it's a cat.' But what if the cat's ears are hidden? What if it's a different breed with different features? What about when the photo is blurry or dark? For every rule you write, you'd need ten more rules to handle the exceptions. Then you'd need rules for the exceptions to those exceptions. The number of rules would explode into the millions, and you still wouldn't cover everything.

This explosion of rules is called combinatorial explosion, and it's why the old approach eventually hit a wall.

Machine Learning completely changed how we think about AI. Instead of writing the rules ourselves, we let the computer discover them.

Here's how it works. Instead of programming rules, we program a learning algorithm. Then we show this algorithm thousands or millions of examples. Want the AI to recognize cats? Show it ten thousand cat photos and ten thousand non-cat photos. The algorithm studies these examples and figures out the patterns on its own.

This is called the data-driven approach because the data drives the learning. The patterns the algorithm discovers are often incredibly complex, sometimes too complex for humans to even describe. But that's okay because the algorithm doesn't need us to describe them. It finds them in the data.

The shift to Machine Learning solved the scalability problem. Once you have a good learning algorithm, you can give it more and more data to improve its performance. This is why today's AI can do things that seemed impossible twenty years ago.

A translation system using the old rule-based approach might handle a few languages poorly. A modern Machine Learning system can translate between over one hundred languages with impressive accuracy. A rule-based image recognition system would struggle with basic shapes. A modern system can identify millions of different objects, faces, and scenes.

The more data you provide, the smarter the system becomes. That's the power of the Machine Learning revolution.

The cycle is iterative: data feeds training, training produces a model, deployment generates feedback, and feedback flows back into the next round of data collection.

Every AI project starts with data. Think of data as the textbook from which the AI will study. Just like you can't learn much from a poorly written textbook, an AI can't learn well from poor quality data.

Data acquisition means collecting examples that represent what you want the AI to learn. If you're building a system to recognize different types of birds, you need thousands of bird photos. But you can't just grab any photos. The collection needs to be balanced, meaning you need roughly the same number of examples for each type of bird. If you have a thousand robin photos but only ten hawk photos, your AI will become an expert at recognizing robins but terrible at recognizing hawks.

ℹ️
The Quality Rule
There's a saying in AI: 'Garbage in, garbage out.' Even the most sophisticated algorithm will produce bad results if it learns from bad data. Quality matters more than quantity.

Labeling: Teaching Right from Wrong
Imagine learning to identify different types of rocks. You might look at a hundred rocks, but unless someone tells you 'this one is granite, this one is limestone,' you'd just be guessing. AI has the same problem.

Data labeling is the process of telling the AI what the correct answer is for each example. For our bird recognition system, this means going through thousands of photos and adding a label to each one: 'robin,' 'hawk,' 'sparrow,' and so on. This labeled data becomes the answer key that the AI uses to check its work while learning.

Labeling is often done by hand, and it's time-consuming. For large projects, teams of people might spend weeks or months labeling data. But this careful work is essential because these labels are what teach the AI to recognize patterns correctly.

The training phase is where the magic happens. This is when the AI actually learns from the labeled examples you've prepared.

During training, the algorithm studies each example and tries to identify the features that make each category unique. What makes a robin different from a hawk? Maybe it's the red breast, the size, the beak shape, or the way the feathers are arranged. The AI discovers these distinguishing features automatically by analyzing thousands of examples.

As the AI looks at more examples, it keeps adjusting its internal understanding. Think of these adjustments as the AI fine-tuning its mental picture of what each bird looks like. The algorithm makes millions of tiny adjustments to get better and better at telling the birds apart. These adjustments happen through mathematical calculations that update the AI's internal weights, which are like the strength of different connections in its decision-making process.

After training is complete, the AI is ready for the real test. This stage is called inference, and it's when the AI encounters new examples it has never seen before.

Inference is what happens when you actually use the AI system. You show it a photo of a bird, and based on everything it learned during training, it makes a prediction about what type of bird it is. The AI isn't looking up the answer in a database. It's applying the patterns it learned to make an educated guess about this new photo.

A well-trained AI system is robust, which means it works well in different situations. It can recognize a robin whether the photo was taken in bright sunlight or cloudy weather, whether the bird is sitting or flying, whether the camera was close or far away. This robustness comes from being trained on diverse, high-quality data.

Topic 5.5: Generative AI and AI Ethics
How AI generates new content and why we need to think carefully about how we use it.

Most of the AI we've discussed so far recognizes things. It looks at a photo and tells you what's in it, or reads a sentence and understands its meaning. But there's another type of AI that does something different. It creates new things.

Generative AI learns patterns from existing content and then uses those patterns to create something new. It works by predicting what should come next in a sequence. If you're generating an image, it predicts the next pixel. If you're writing text, it predicts the next word. If you're composing music, it predicts the next note.

These systems study millions of examples to understand the underlying patterns and style of the content. An AI that generates paintings learns about color combinations, brush strokes, and composition by analyzing thousands of real paintings. Then it can create new paintings that follow similar patterns but have never existed before.

As AI becomes more common in our daily lives, we need to think carefully about how it's built and used. One of the biggest concerns is something called algorithmic bias.

Remember that AI learns from data, and that data comes from the real world. If the real world has unfairness in it, the AI can learn that unfairness too. For example, if an AI is trained on historical hiring data from a company that mostly hired men for technical positions, the AI might learn to prefer male candidates even though gender shouldn't matter for the job.

This happens because the AI doesn't understand fairness or equality. It just finds patterns in the data. If the data contains human prejudices, the AI will learn those prejudices and might even make them worse. This is why the people who build AI systems need to be very careful about the data they use and regularly check their systems for unfair patterns.

Generative AI has become so good at creating realistic content that it can be hard to tell what's real and what's artificial. You might have heard about deepfakes, which are videos where AI has been used to make someone appear to say or do something they never actually did.

These fake videos are created by AI systems that learned from thousands of real videos. They can copy someone's facial movements, voice, and mannerisms so well that the result looks genuine. While this technology has some positive uses, like in movies and games, it also creates serious problems.

Fake content can be used to spread false information, damage someone's reputation, or trick people into believing things that never happened. This makes it harder to know what to trust online. As this technology becomes more common, we're developing new tools to detect fake content and working on rules about how it can be used responsibly.

Training powerful AI systems requires enormous amounts of data, and sometimes that data includes personal information about real people. This raises important questions about privacy.

When companies collect data to train AI, they need to think about consent. Did people agree to have their information used this way? Is the data being protected properly? Could the AI accidentally reveal private information about individuals?

Responsible AI development means putting safety and privacy first. It means being transparent about how AI systems work, what data they use, and what they're capable of doing. The goal is to create AI that helps people while respecting their rights and protecting them from harm.

Topic 5.6a: Training Our First Model
In this lab, you'll train your very own AI model using a tool called Teachable Machine from Google. This tool makes it easy to experience the complete process of building AI without needing to write complex code. You'll see firsthand how AI learns from examples, and you'll understand why the quality and variety of your training data matters so much.

There's an important concept in AI called robustness. A robust system is one that works well across different conditions and different people. Right now, you might be the only person in your training photos. This creates a potential problem.

If all your training examples show your face from the same angle, in the same lighting, at the same distance from the camera, the AI might learn your specific face rather than learning what emotions look like in general. To build a robust system, you need to introduce variety into your training data. Try recording emotions while facing slightly left, then slightly right. Record some examples close to the camera and some farther away. If the lighting in your room changes throughout the day, record examples in different lighting conditions. ℹ️
The Diversity Principle
More variety in your training data leads to an AI that generalizes better to new situations. This is one of the most important principles in machine learning.

Remember how important it was to have roughly the same number of thumbs up and thumbs down examples? That principle becomes even more critical when you have four categories instead of two.

Imagine you record five hundred examples of happy, four hundred of sad, three hundred of angry, and only one hundred of shocked. What do you think will happen? The AI will become really good at recognizing happy expressions because it has so many examples to learn from. But it will struggle with shocked expressions because it hasn't seen enough examples to learn the pattern well.

Even worse, when the AI is uncertain about what it's seeing, it might default to guessing happy simply because that was the most common category in the training data. This is called class imbalance bias. The AI develops a bias toward the categories that appeared most frequently during training.

The solution is simple but requires patience. Aim for roughly the same number of examples for each emotion. If you collect two hundred examples of happy, try to collect close to two hundred examples of sad, angry, and shocked as well. Balanced data creates fair learning opportunities for all categories.

Now comes the solution phase. Unlike traditional programming where you'd edit code to fix bugs, AI problems are almost always fixed by improving the training data. This process is called data augmentation, which means adding carefully chosen examples that address the specific weaknesses you identified.

7
If emotions are getting confused: Go back to Teachable Machine and add new examples that exaggerate the differences between those specific emotions.
Let's say sad and angry keep getting mixed up. Think about what makes these emotions visually different. For sad, emphasize the downturned mouth corners and drooping eyelids. Make your face look genuinely defeated. For angry, really furrow your eyebrows, tense your jaw, and create that intense expression. The key word is exaggerate. You're not trying to show subtle emotions. You're teaching the AI the clearest possible version of each category.

8
Record at least fifty new examples for each confused emotion, making the distinctive features very obvious.
Why fifty? Because you need enough examples to shift the AI's learned patterns significantly. Adding just ten examples to a dataset of three hundred won't make much difference. But adding fifty examples that all emphasize the key differences will strengthen those specific features in the AI's understanding.

9
If lighting affects your model: Add training examples in different lighting conditions.
Turn off some lights in your room and record examples. Move near a window and record examples. Try recording with the light source behind you, then in front of you. Each lighting condition shows the AI that emotions look the same even when the overall brightness changes. You're teaching the model to ignore lighting and focus on facial features.

10
If angles affect your model: Record examples with your head tilted and turned at different angles.
Tilt your head slightly to the left while making each expression. Then tilt to the right. Turn your head at a slight angle away from the camera. Each angle variation teaches the AI that the emotion stays the same even when your spatial orientation changes. The AI learns to recognize the emotion from any viewpoint.

Rule 1
The Targeted Addition Rule
Only add new examples that specifically address the problem you diagnosed. Random examples don't help.
Rule 2
The Exaggeration Principle
When fixing confusion between categories, make the differences as obvious as possible in new training data.
Rule 3
The Variety Balance Rule
Add variety in conditions (lighting, angles) but keep the emotion expression consistent within each category.

After adding new examples, click Train Model in Teachable Machine and wait for training to complete.
The training process looks the same as before, but something different is happening inside. The AI is now processing your original examples plus all the new targeted examples you added. The neural network's weights are being adjusted to account for these new patterns. Where before the boundary between sad and angry was fuzzy, now it should become clearer because you gave the AI more evidence of what makes them different.

Download the new keras_model.h5 file and the new labels.txt file. In your Google Colab notebook, delete the old files and upload these new ones. Make sure they have exactly the same filenames as before, because your Python code looks for those specific names.

Run your Python inference code again and test the improved model.
Now when you test each emotion, you should see improvement in the specific areas you addressed. If you added examples with head tilts, test with head tilts. If you added examples in different lighting, test in different lighting. The improvements should be visible exactly where you focused your data augmentation efforts.

Compare your new confidence scores to the ones you wrote down in Step 2. Look for improvement.
You're looking for specific changes. Did the emotions that were getting confused now have higher, more stable confidence scores? Did the flickering stop or reduce significantly? If you see improvement, your data augmentation strategy worked. If you don't see improvement, you might need to add even more examples or reconsider whether you're truly exaggerating the distinctive features enough.

🔬
Iteration is Normal
Professional AI engineers go through this cycle many times. The first round of data augmentation rarely solves everything. You might need to repeat steps seven through fourteen multiple times, each time targeting remaining weaknesses.

There's one technical issue that can make your model appear broken even when it's working perfectly. It has to do with how Python matches prediction numbers to emotion names. Let's walk through this carefully because it's a common source of frustration.

15
Open your labels.txt file in a text editor and look at the order of the emotion names.
You should see four lines, one for each emotion. Maybe it looks like this: Happy, Sad, Angry, Shocked. The order matters enormously because Python reads this file line by line and assigns each line a number starting from zero. The first line becomes index zero, the second line becomes index one, the third line becomes index two, and the fourth line becomes index three.

16
Remember the order you created the classes in Teachable Machine. Write it down.
When you first set up your project in Teachable Machine, you created four classes for your four emotions. The order you created them in is crucial. Let's say you created them in this order: Happy first, then Sad, then Angry, then Shocked. Teachable Machine internally numbered these as zero, one, two, and three respectively.

17
Make absolutely sure your labels.txt file lists emotions in the exact same order you created the classes.
Here's what happens if they don't match. Imagine you trained the model with Happy as class zero, but your labels.txt file lists Angry first. When your Python code gets a prediction of index zero, it looks at labels.txt and reads the first line, which says Angry. So even though the AI correctly identified Happy (class zero), your program displays Angry because that's what's on line zero of the text file. The AI is right but the labels are wrong.

Teachable Machine Order
Happy (0), Sad (1), Angry (2), Shocked (3)
labels.txt MUST Match
Happy (line 0), Sad (line 1), Angry (line 2), Shocked (line 3)
What AI Predicts
Index number (0, 1, 2, or 3)
What Python Displays
Whatever text is at that line number in labels.txt
18
If predictions seem completely random or backwards, fix your labels.txt file order and re-upload it to Colab.
You don't need to retrain the model. You don't need to change any code. Just fix the order of lines in labels.txt so they match the order you created classes in Teachable Machine. Save the file, delete the old one from Colab, upload the corrected one, and run your inference code again. The predictions should now make sense.

You've gone through the diagnostic process, fixed problems through data augmentation, and ensured your labels are aligned correctly. Now it's time to evaluate your final model properly.

19
Test each emotion ten times in a row. Record whether the prediction was correct each time.
This gives you a real accuracy percentage. If happy was correctly identified nine out of ten times, that's ninety percent accuracy for the happy category. Do this for all four emotions. Your overall model accuracy is the average across all four categories.

20
Calculate your accuracy: (Number of correct predictions) divided by (Total predictions) times 100.
Let's say you made forty total predictions across the four emotions, and thirty-four were correct. That's thirty-four divided by forty, which equals zero point eight five. Multiply by one hundred to get eighty-five percent accuracy. This is a solid result for a student project.

Test your model on someone else. Ask a friend or family member to make the expressions.
This is the ultimate test of generalization. If your model works on someone else's face, it learned the general concept of emotions rather than just memorizing your specific face. If it fails on another person, your model overfitted to your appearance. The solution would be to add training examples from multiple different people, teaching the AI that emotions look similar across different faces.
